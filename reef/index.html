1 Reef vs. freshwater — what actually changes

You already run a big pond, so you know cycling, husbandry, and reading water. A reef isn't harder so much as it's stricter about stability and adds a few disciplines freshwater never asked of you. Here's the translation.

You already know this → nitrogen cycle, patience, slow stocking, watching animals for behavior, not overfeeding, big-water-volume-is-forgiving. All of it carries straight over.

The genuinely new stuff

  • Salinity is a parameter you now own. Target 35 ppt / SG 1.026, measured with a refractometer. It must stay steady — you top off evaporation with fresh RO/DI (the salt stays behind), not saltwater. An auto-top-off (ATO) does this for you.
  • The rock is the filter. Live rock + sand host the bacteria — there's no pond-style canister doing the biology. This is the "Berlin method." Your sump adds a skimmer, heater, and space, not a filter cartridge.
  • A protein skimmer. No freshwater equivalent. It strips dissolved organics out as foam before they rot into nitrate/phosphate. On a reef it runs 24/7 and is not optional.
  • Light is food, not just viewing. Corals photosynthesize — the LED spectrum and intensity (PAR) literally feed them. This is the biggest mental shift from freshwater plants.
  • Strong, turbulent flow. Reefs want way more current than a pond — wavemakers keep corals clean and fed. Think "surge," not "gentle."
  • The chemistry trio: Alkalinity, Calcium, Magnesium. Growing corals consume these to build skeleton, so you test and replenish them. This is the new weekly rhythm; on a small first reef it's easy, and you scale up dosing only as coral demand grows.
  • Quarantine matters far more. Marine ich and velvet are faster and deadlier than anything in freshwater. A cheap bare quarantine tub for new fish is the single best insurance a first reef can buy.
  • RO/DI water, always. Even your soft Hetch Hetchy tap carries chloramine and trace phosphate/silicate that feed nuisance algae. More on why your water is actually a head start below.
Your Pacifica water is a gift. Hetch Hetchy is very soft / low-TDS, so your RO/DI unit barely has to work — the membrane lasts longer and hits 0 TDS easily. You still run everything through RO/DI (soft ≠ clean of chloramine and nutrients), but you're starting from an easier place than most reefers.
Your cool, foggy coast is another gift. Reef tanks fight heat, not cold — pumps and lights dump warmth, and most reefers battle summer highs with chillers. In Pacifica's mild ~55–65°F rooms, your heaters do the work and you can very likely skip a chiller entirely. That's real money and noise saved.

2 Tank size & fitting the room

Two rules pull against each other: bigger water is more stable (more forgiving of a beginner's mistakes — you know this from the pond), but a condo has a floor. The sweet spot for a first reef showpiece lands around a 4–5 foot tank.

(2) That west sun is the one real reef wrinkle — see the note below.
Managing the west window (evening sun). Uncontrolled sunlight is the classic fuel for nuisance algae in a young reef and can add unpredictable heat — on a reef your LEDs feed the corals, so sun is a wildcard to tame, especially in year one. Three reasons it's easily managed here: the tank sits perpendicular to the window, so sun rakes the short end/side, not your main (south-facing) viewing pane — little glare where it counts; your existing sheers plus a simple light-filtering shade for the strongest late-day hours handle the first year (lean into the gorgeous evening glow once the reef is mature); and a temperature controller with a phone alert covers the rare hot, clear evening. Not a dealbreaker — just a "design around it" detail.

How to think about the size choice

  • Don't go tiny to "start safe." Nano reefs (<30g) swing fast and punish beginners. More water = slower swings = more forgiving. A ~90–150g is genuinely an easier first reef than a 20g.
  • Match length to the wall and the view. A 5-foot tank at dining-table eye level is a stunning "dive site on the wall." Measure the wall, leave room to open the cabinet, and picture where you'll sit and look in.
  • Height: 24″ is plenty. Very tall/deep tanks get hard to reach into and need punchier (pricier) light to drive corals on the bottom. Standard ~24″ height keeps maintenance humane.
Condo reality check — floor loading. A filled ~120g system (water + rock + sand + stand + tank) runs roughly 1,400–1,600 lb over a small footprint. That's usually fine, but it's worth doing right: place the tank along a load-bearing wall and ideally perpendicular across the floor joists (spreads the load over several joists), ground floor if you have the option. If you're on an upper floor, a quick look at how the joists run — or one text to a contractor — is cheap peace of mind before buying. Tell me the room and floor and I'll help you reason through it.
Two more dining-room touches. (1) Quiet. You'll eat next to this thing — spec DC-controllable pumps and a quiet skimmer so it hums, not roars. (2) Earthquake + water. It's California and it's a wood floor: a level, secured stand, a drip tray / leak sensor under the cabinet, and an ATO with a failsafe keep a small "oops" from becoming a floor repair.

3 Equipment list

Grouped by job. Quantities/sizing assume the ~120g starting point. We'll pick exact models when you're ready — this is the shopping map.

The core system

  • Display tank + stand + sump — low-iron rimless, drilled for an overflow, with a sump in the cabinet (holds the skimmer, heaters, return pump, and a refugium).
  • Live rock + sand — the biological filter and your aquascape. ~1–1.5 lb rock/gal and a shallow sand bed. Dry/cured rock cycled up is cleanest (no pest hitchhikers); a little live rock or "bio-brick" seeds the bacteria.
  • Return pump — a quiet DC pump pushing water sump→display.
  • Wavemakers / powerheads — 2× controllable for turbulent reef flow.

Life support

  • Protein skimmer — sized a bit above your volume; the heart of nutrient control.
  • Heater(s) + controller — two smaller heaters (redundancy) on a separate temperature controller so one stuck heater can't cook the tank.
  • Reef LED lighting — 2–3 quality tunable fixtures (e.g. Radion / AI Hydra / Reefi class) for coral-driving spectrum and PAR, on a slow sunrise/sunset schedule.
  • Auto top-off (ATO) — replaces evaporated water with RO/DI automatically to hold salinity steady. Get one with a redundant/float failsafe.

Water & testing (your new water station)

  • RO/DI unit — makes your source water. Your soft tap means long membrane life.
  • Salt mix + mixing station — a brute can + small pump + heater to pre-mix and store saltwater and fresh top-off.
  • Refractometer — the one true salinity tool (calibrate with fluid).
  • Test kits — Alkalinity, Calcium, Magnesium, Nitrate, Phosphate (plus ammonia during cycle). A Hanna checker or two for alk/phosphate is worth it.

Grow-into-it (add later, not day one)

  • Dosing pumps — automate alk/cal/mag once corals grow enough to need it.
  • Aquarium controller (e.g. Apex) — monitors temp/level/leaks, controls pumps and lights, and texts you if something's wrong. For a living-room showpiece, this is the "sleep at night" upgrade — great second-phase buy.
  • Quarantine tub — cheap, bare, for new fish. Buy it before your first fish.

5 Cycling — same idea, saltwater flavor

The nitrogen cycle you know from the pond is exactly the same here: ammonia → nitrite → nitrate, driven by bacteria colonizing the rock. What's different is where it lives (the rock, not a filter) and a very normal ugly phase on the way to clear.

Step 1 · set the stage

Salt in, rock in, flow & heat on

Fill with RO/DI mixed to 35 ppt / SG 1.026, heat to ~78°F, aquascape your rock, run the pumps and skimmer. No light needed yet (light now just grows algae).

Step 2 · seed & feed the bacteria

Add a bacteria starter + an ammonia source

Bottled nitrifying bacteria plus a small ammonia source (or a raw shrimp, or live rock doing it naturally) gets the colony going. Then you wait and test — just like the pond, patience is the tool.

Step 3 · watch the numbers turn over

Ammonia spikes, then nitrite, then both hit zero

Usually ~2–6 weeks. When a dose of ammonia clears to 0 ammonia & 0 nitrite within a day and you just have some nitrate, the biofilter is alive. Do a water change to knock nitrate down.

Step 4 · ride out the "ugly stage"

Diatoms & a little algae — totally normal

A brown diatom film (then maybe some hair algae or cyano) blooms as a young tank finds balance. This rattles new reefers, but it's expected and temporary. Don't nuke it — stay patient, keep nutrients in check, and let a cleanup crew and time settle it. This is the phase where your pond-keeper patience is a superpower.

Step 5 · cleanup crew, then first fish

Snails/hermits first; one or two hardy fish next

A cleanup crew (CUC) grazes the algae as the tank matures. A week or two later, add your first hardy fish (a clownfish is the classic — bulletproof and full of character). Slowly.

6 The "first reef, done right" roadmap

Deliberately unhurried. Each phase earns the next. Most first-reef heartbreak comes from rushing phases 2–4 — and rushing is the opposite of how you like to do things.

Phase 0 · now — learn & decide

Lock the vision

Pick the tank size & spot (floor-load check), decide budget tier, and settle the look you want (which of your dives are we recreating?). Nothing bought — just clarity.

Phase 1 · weeks 1–2 — the water muscle

Stand up the gear & the water station

Tank/stand/sump in place and leak-tested, RO/DI and mixing station running, salt mixed, heaters and skimmer on. Get comfortable making and storing water before livestock depend on it.

Phase 2 · weeks 2–8 — aquascape & cycle

Build the reef structure, then cycle it

Design an open, stable rockscape with caves and flow paths (this is your dive site's bones). Cycle fully. Ride the ugly stage. No shortcuts here.

Phase 3 · month 2–3 — first life

Cleanup crew, then a hardy fish or two

Dial in stability over a few weeks of small water changes and testing. Prove you can hold salinity, temp, and nutrients steady. Set up the quarantine tub now.

Phase 4 · month 3–5 — first corals

Easy softies & LPS, lights ramped low

Start with forgiving corals — zoanthids, mushrooms, leathers, then hammer/frogspawn/torch (euphyllia) and acans. Begin the weekly alk/cal/mag testing rhythm. Watch how each coral responds before adding more.

Phase 5 · month 6+ — grow the reef

Stock slowly toward the dream

Add fish a few at a time (all quarantined), let the coral garden fill in, and only much later — once the tank is mature and rock-steady — consider the fussier SPS corals. Add the controller/dosing upgrade when coral demand and your comfort say it's time.

The one habit that makes or breaks a first reef: a light, boring, consistent weekly routine — test, small water change, top-off check, glance at every animal. You already do exactly this with the pond. Reef rewards the patient keeper, and that's you.

7 Stocking — your dive site (the fun chapter, later)

This is where we'll spend real time once the tank is cycling — and where "feels like my favorite dives" gets specific. A taste of the direction, tuned to how you pick animals (character and wellbeing over spec-sheet flash):

Fish with personality (your kind of fish)

  • A clownfish pair — the iconic reef bond, especially hosting an anemone (added later, once the tank is mature and your light is up to it). Endless character.
  • Wrasses — busy, curious, brilliantly colored, and each one an individual — very much a "character" fish.
  • Gobies & blennies — comedians of the reef; a shrimp-goby pair sharing a burrow is pure dive-site charm.
  • A tang (blue/yellow) — the postcard dive fish, but they need swimming room, so it's a reason a 5-footer earns its keep. One, added thoughtfully.
  • A centerpiece with attitude — e.g. a dwarf angel or a mandarin (mandarin much later, once the tank grows enough live food). We'll choose the one fish that greets you at the glass.

Corals — the living structure

Start with hardy, beautiful softies and LPS that sway in the flow (the movement is a huge part of the "underwater" feeling), and build a garden of color and texture. SPS (the classic branching Acropora "reef" look) comes much later, as a mature-tank reward — not a first-year goal.
